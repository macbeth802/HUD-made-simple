Lesson 2: Core accounting concepts

What is net position?

In simple terms, net position is what the authority owns minus what it owes: assets minus liabilities. It takes the place of owner's equity, and it is split into three parts.

ComponentWhat it meansExample
Net investment in capital assetsThe value of land, buildings, and equipment (after depreciation) minus the debt used to buy or build them. This money is tied up in property.A building worth $3,000,000 after depreciation, with $500,000 of debt on it, contributes $2,500,000.
Restricted net positionAssets that outside rules limit to specific uses.$200,000 of housing assistance payment (HAP) funds that can only pay voucher assistance.
Unrestricted net positionEverything else, available for general use under the authority's policies.$400,000 of other cash minus $100,000 of other liabilities leaves $300,000.

Net position = net investment in capital assets + restricted + unrestricted. In the example, $2,500,000 + $200,000 + $300,000 = $3,000,000. These three lines appear on the statement of net position in the AFS and again in the FDS.

Older documents may call these "net assets," with names like "invested in capital assets, net of related debt," "restricted net assets," and "unrestricted net assets." Current standards use "net position," so you will see both in the wild.

How fund accounting differs from business accounting

If you have worked as a bookkeeper for a business, most of the mechanics will feel familiar: debits and credits, accrual accounting, and financial statements. The difference is in what the books are for and how they are organized. A business asks, "Did we make a profit?" A housing authority asks, "Did we use each source of money the way we were supposed to?"

TopicTraditional business accountingFund accounting in a housing authority
Main purposeMeasure profit for the ownersShow that money was used as intended (stewardship and accountability)
Structure of the booksOne set of books for the whole company, sometimes with tags or classes for departmentsA separate, self-balancing set of accounts for each program or fund, each with its own assets, liabilities, revenues, and expenses
Equity sectionOwner's equity and retained earningsNet position, usually shown as net investment in capital assets, restricted, and unrestricted
Restrictions on moneyOwners decide how profits are usedFunding sources (HUD, the IRS, states) limit what each dollar can pay for, so money is tracked by source
BudgetA planning toolA control: spending is compared against approved budgets and funding limits
Moving money between unitsTransfers between departments are internal and usually invisibleEvery transfer is recorded as a due to / due from balance between programs, and must be tracked and settled
ReportingFinancial statements for the owners, lenders, and tax returnsThe AFS for the whole authority, plus the FDS by program for HUD, plus program reports to each funder

A worked example

The central office pays a $1,000 invoice for maintenance supplies used at a public housing property. A business bookkeeper would record one expense and one payment. In a housing authority, the $1,000 expense belongs to the Public Housing program because that is where the supplies were used, but the cash came from the Central Office Cost Center. The books record the expense in Public Housing and set up a due to / due from balance between the two, so each unit stays balanced and the amount can be settled later.

Practice: Why would it be a problem if the housing authority used Housing Choice Voucher administrative fee money to pay for a Public Housing expense without recording it?

Rules on moving money between programs and on how net position is presented come from HUD and from governmental accounting standards, and they can change. Check current HUD guidance and ask your auditor before setting up interprogram transactions.
